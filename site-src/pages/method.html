How a review is done

Each review reads a national report on its own terms first, then checks what it assumed and aimed for against the evidence since. Generative AI does most of the reading and drafting, in the same steps for every report. The result is a starting point for discussion, not a final word.

Version 0.2 · 18 Nov 2026 · Draft for review

01

A review is a conversation starter, not a verdict

Governments publish long-range visions and scenarios to steer a country for decades. They are argued over at launch, then rarely read again. Norantz reads them again, at a date, and asks what has held.

A review is meant to be used in two ways:

  • To start a wider conversation. It gives anyone, not only specialists, a short and sourced way into a national plan: what it hoped for, what it assumed, and where things stand now.
  • As a starting point for deeper, human-led work. It gathers the report's claims, the latest official figures, and the changes since publication in one place. Advisers, researchers, journalists, and the report's own authors can take it from there: test it, challenge it, and add what it misses.

What a review is not

  • Not a ranking of countries or a score for a government. There is no overall score.
  • Not a forecast. It says where things stand at a date, and what has changed.
  • Not an audit or an evaluation of a programme. It does not judge whether officials did their jobs.
  • Not advice. It does not say what a government should do now.
  • Not final. It is drafted by AI, may contain errors, and is updated as data and corrections arrive.

02

Generative AI drafts each review; people check it, and errors remain possible

Every review on this site is drafted with generative AI: large language models that read text and write text. We use AI because it can read long reports in their own language, apply the same steps to every report, and repeat the work when new data comes out. That makes it possible to review many national plans, not just a few.

AI also makes mistakes, and some of them look convincing. Please read every review with that in mind.

What the AI does

  • Reads the report and logs its claims, goals, and commitments, each with a page reference.
  • Proposes the assumptions the report relies on but does not state.
  • Searches official sources for the latest figures and records each with its date and link.
  • Lists what has changed since publication and what each change bears on.
  • Proposes a reading for each item and drafts the page.

What people do

  • Confirm what kind of report it is and whether it is still in force.
  • Check the assumptions the AI says are implied, and any indicator the AI proposes for a goal with no number.
  • Choose which themes to review first.
  • Review every reading, above all "not supported so far" and "behind", before publication.
  • Read the whole page before it goes live, and handle corrections and replies afterwards.

Each page says, step by step, what the AI did and what a person checked, and names the person. Where a check has not yet been done, the page says so.

[TRIAL] Proposed rule: nothing is published until a named person has signed off the readings and the page. Today no rating on either pilot page has been reviewed by a person.

The kinds of error to look out for

ErrorWhat it looks likeWhat we do about it
Misread figureA number copied wrongly, or read off a chart as if it were exact.Figures are copied from the text layer of the source. Anything read off a chart is marked "~" and never used for a reading.
Wrong referenceA page number or a source link that does not say what the review claims.Every claim carries a page reference and every figure a link, so a reader can check in one step.
Outdated or mismatched dataAn older figure where a newer one exists, or a series with a changed definition.Every figure has an "as of" date. Changes of definition are logged as limits.
Second-hand figuresAn official figure taken from a news report or think tank, not the original.Marked "verify at source" until checked against the original publication.
Over-confident readingA firm label on thin evidence, or a reading that goes beyond what the data shows.What would challenge each item is written before any evidence is gathered. People review every reading.
Missing contextA change that matters locally but is absent from the sources searched.The sources searched are fixed and listed, so gaps can be seen. Authors and readers can add context.
TranslationA term in the report's language given the wrong sense in English.Key quotes are given in the original language beside the translation.

If you find an error, please tell us. Corrections are made on the page, dated, and listed in the changelog.

04

A vision and a scenario set are read differently

A vision describes one desired future and a route to it. Its goals are the authors' aims, not forecasts. The review asks whether what the vision assumed has held, and where things stand against its own path.

A scenario set describes several possible futures and does not choose one. Such reports usually say they are not predictions. The review does not ask whether a scenario "came true". It asks which forces mattered, which elements can be seen, and whether events stayed inside the range the scenarios covered.

Some reports do both: they set out several futures and argue for one. España 2050 is this kind. Its preferred future is read as a vision, and the others as comparison paths.

Three facts recorded about every report

Kind
A vision, a scenario set, or a vision with comparison scenarios.
Numbers
Quantified (dated targets or modelled paths), partly quantified, or qualitative. A goal with no number gets one to three proposed indicators, each labelled as the reviewer's and checked by a person, or is marked "not measurable".
Standing
The force the report had when published (law, executive order, adopted policy, advisory, non-government) and its status today (in force, revised, superseded, lapsed). A superseded report gets a shorter review: its targets are shown with their last-known status and marked "no longer in force", and its successor is named.

The kind is decided from the report's own text, not from descriptions of it, and a person confirms it.

05

Everything rests on a register of what the report said

Each review starts by logging the report's contents as a register: one line per item, in the report's own terms, with its page reference. Each item is one of four kinds, because a goal not yet reached is not the same as an assumption that did not hold.

KindWhat it is
ClaimA statement about the world: a fact, a trend, a projection, or a cause-and-effect assumption.
GoalA desired end state or a dated target.
CommitmentA measure, reform, institution, or spending line the report says will be put in place.
SignpostAn observable sign that a scenario is unfolding (scenario sets only).

For each item the register also records whether the report states it or only implies it, what it depends on, and what kind of evidence would challenge it. That last field is written before any evidence is gathered, so the test is set before the answer is known.

Assumptions visions often leave unstated

Visions are written to build support, so the conditions they need are often left out. Every vision is checked for these:

  • Enabling conditions: what the outside world must provide, such as world growth, open trade, finance, and a stable climate.
  • Growth path: what rate of growth, for how long, the end state needs.
  • Causal links: which measures are meant to produce each goal, and how.
  • State capacity: whether the state can fund, staff, and carry out the measures.
  • Continuity: whether the vision is assumed to survive changes of government.
  • Baselines: which population, workforce, or resource projections it takes as given.
  • No large shock: whether the path allows for interruption.

Nine steps for a vision, each with a named human check

1. IntakeRecords what the report is, who published it, and whether it is still in force.The kind of report and its standing
2. RegisterLogs every claim, goal, and commitment with its page reference; runs the unstated-assumption checklist and a search for topics the report does not cover.Implied assumptions and proposed indicators
3. ConsistencyFlags places where the report's own figures disagree.Which figure to test against
4. ThemesGroups items into themes and ranks the assumptions most goals rest on.Which themes to review, in what order

6. What has changedLists major shifts, trends, and emerging issues since publication, and what each bears on.The list, and the direction of each link
7. ReadingsApplies the labels below and tests the rate each target now needs.Every reading, above all "not supported so far" and "behind"

Eight steps for a scenario set

The same shape, with three differences. The register logs the forces the scenarios turn on, and the signposts for each scenario. Forces are ranked by how many scenarios turn on each. And step 6 runs four tests:

TestQuestion
ForcesDid the forces the report named turn out to matter? Did those it treated as near-certain behave as described?
SignpostsFor each scenario, which signposts can be seen?
RangeDid major developments fall inside the range the scenarios spanned, at its edge, or outside it?
ImplicationsWhere the report gives advice, does it hold across what happened?

No scenario is named as "the" outcome unless most of its elements agree. A scenario set with no full public text is not reviewed.

[TRIAL] The scenario-set steps are a first draft and have not been run on any report.

07

Readings rest on official sources only

SourceExamplesUse
Tier 1National statistics offices, central banks, finance ministries, Eurostat, IMF, World Bank, WTO, ILO, APOSupports a reading
Tier 2Think tanks and research institutesContext and trends; emerging issues, labelled as such
MediaNews reportsNever supports a reading. May point to a candidate, or carry an official figure that is then checked at source.
  • Every figure carries an "as of" date and a link.
  • Where a plan's starting figure for a year was itself a projection, the published outturn replaces it, and both are shown.
  • A figure taken from a second-hand account of an official source is marked "verify at source" until checked.

08

What has changed since publication is read against the report's own goals

The evidence looks back: has each assumption held so far? A second pass looks around: what is new in the country's situation since the report was written, and what does it mean for the report?

Kind of changeMeaningEvidence needed
Major shiftA break that has already happened, with a date.Tier 1
TrendA measurable direction over several years that began, sped up, slowed, or reversed since publication.Tier 1, or Tier 2 with data
Emerging issueAn early development whose size and direction are still unclear.Tier 2 allowed; labelled as emerging

Candidates come from a fixed set of sources, so two runs on the same report give similar lists: the country's own later plans and progress reports, later foresight and risk reports from recognised regional and international bodies, and topics the report does not cover. Eight areas are always searched, and each returns at least one candidate or an explicit "none found": demographic and social; economic and trade; technology; environment and climate; domestic politics and institutions; geopolitics and security; fiscal and financial; international rules and regulation.

Each change is linked to the items it bears on, with a one-sentence mechanism. A change is a challenge if it makes one of the report's own goals harder to reach and an opportunity if it makes one easier, whatever the reviewer thinks of the goal. Whether the report mentions the change is recorded as a fact, not as a judgement on whether its authors should have foreseen it.

09

Whether a target is still reachable is worked out, not guessed

For each dated target with a number, four rates are compared:

  1. the rate of change the target needed from the year of publication;
  2. the rate achieved since;
  3. the rate now needed from the latest figure to reach the target on time;
  4. the best rate the country has kept up over a similar period on record.

The finding is stated plainly, for example: "reaching the target now needs a rate above the best ten-year rate on record". That is a statement about arithmetic, not about effort.

10

What each label means

Colour tells time: Loam is what the report said, then; Sky is what the evidence shows, now. Shape tells status, always with a word. Dotted lines are planned; solid lines are observed. There is no red, amber, or green.

How a claim has fared so far
HoldsThe evidence matches what the report expected.
Goes furtherThings moved further in the direction the report expected.
Holds in partThe direction holds; the size or timing does not.
Not supported so farThe evidence so far runs against what the report expected.
Not yet reviewedToo early to test, or not yet checked.
Where a target figure stands against the report's own path
AheadBetter than the path for its year.
On pathClose to the path.
BehindBelow the path. This describes a distance from the plan, not a government.
MetThe target has been reached.
No longer in forceThe target belongs to a report since replaced; its last-known status is shown.
What new conditions may mean from here
Under pressureEstablished changes push against the claim.
SupportedEstablished changes run with it.
MixedEstablished changes pull both ways.
WatchOnly emerging signs so far; not yet clear which way.
UntouchedNothing found that bears on it.
Recommendations
Still standsThe reasoning behind it still holds.
Worth revisitingAn assumption it rests on has moved.
Revisit: new conditionsAn assumption it rests on is under pressure from an established change, even if the data has not yet moved.

11

Counts are shown by label, never as a score

A page may count its items by label: for example, 11 items hold and 5 are not supported so far. Those counts always sit beside how much of the report has been reviewed and how many items are not yet reviewed. They are never added up into a score, a percentage, or a grade, and countries are never ranked.

12

Reviews are dated, versioned, and updated

Each review shows the date its evidence runs to ("reviewed as of") and the date of its next planned update, usually after the country's main annual statistics are released. Each published version keeps its own fixed address, so a citation always points to what the reader saw. See corrections and changelog.

13

The method has limits, and each page lists its own

  • It can only test what can be measured. Goals with no number rely on indicators the reviewer proposes.
  • Official statistics arrive late and are revised. A reading can change when they do.
  • A gap between a plan and the data does not say why the gap exists. The review does not assign cause or blame.
  • The sources searched for changes are fixed, so something important outside them can be missed.
  • Generative AI can misread, mistranslate, or overstate, as set out above. Human checks reduce this but do not remove it.

Every review ends with a limits log: secondary sources used, sources that could not be opened, unverified figures, and every indicator the reviewer proposed.
